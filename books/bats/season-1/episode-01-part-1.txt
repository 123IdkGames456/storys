BATS: ZOMBIE APOCALYPSE
SEASON 1 — THE BEGINNING
EPISODE 1 — BEFORE EVERYTHING
PART 1

The city never really went quiet.

Even before sunrise, delivery trucks rattled over the roads, buses sighed at empty stops, and the signs above the shops buzzed as if they were trying to remember what they were advertising. From the roof of a narrow office building, Bats watched the streets wake up.

He was sixteen. His dark fur was untidy from the wind, and one ear kept folding sideways whenever a gust came between the buildings. His wings were tucked behind him. They were the part of himself he liked best, although he rarely said so. Flying made the city feel smaller. It made every locked door and crowded street seem temporary.

His phone vibrated.

A message from Varek: MEETING ROOM. NOW.

Bats typed: GOOD MORNING TO YOU TOO.

Three dots appeared, vanished, then appeared again.

Varek: Now, Bats.

“Charming,” Bats muttered.

He stepped away from the edge and opened his wings. For a second he stayed still, listening to the wind. Then he jumped.

The first few beats carried him above the neighbouring rooftops. He glided between two buildings, rose over a traffic light and crossed the street before the cars below had finished waiting at the red signal. He could have taken the lift, or the stairs, or the train like everyone else. He flew because he could.

That was one of the few things about his life that still felt like his own.

The organization’s headquarters occupied an unremarkable building in the business district. There was no sign outside, only a grey entrance and windows that reflected the sky. People walked past without looking twice.

Bats landed in a service alley, folded his wings and entered through the side door. A receptionist looked up from her screen.

“You’re late.”

“I’m beginning to think everyone rehearsed that line.”

“You are late.”

“Then the rehearsal was successful.”

She pointed towards the lift.

The meeting room was on the sixth floor. Varek stood beside a large table, reading something on a tablet. He was a broad-shouldered wolf with a neat coat, a quiet voice and a talent for making a room feel colder without touching the thermostat.

“You took your time,” he said.

“I travelled here under my own power. Very old-fashioned.”

“Sit down.”

Bats sat. A folder waited in front of him.

The organization did not use one public name. Its members called it the Network, though the name was more habit than title. It arranged private jobs for people who were willing to work outside ordinary rules. Bats had been drawn into it when he was younger. He had been good at noticing details, staying calm and remembering what people said. Someone had noticed those qualities and offered him a place.

At first, he had liked the structure. There were schedules, training rooms, people who seemed to know exactly what they were doing. He had been told he was unusually capable. He had mistaken that for being valued.

Over time, he learned the difference.

The Network valued results. It did not always value the people expected to produce them.

Bats opened the folder. Varek explained that the assignment had changed since the first briefing. There were new names, a different location and a tighter time window. Bats listened, then read the pages himself.

“This isn't the same plan,” he said.

“No.”

“You said it was straightforward.”

“I said the original assignment was straightforward.”

“That is a very convenient difference.”

Varek ignored the comment. “You are to follow the updated instructions. If the situation changes in a way you cannot account for, you leave.”

Bats looked up.

“You're telling me to leave?”

“I am telling you not to force a bad situation into becoming a worse one.”

“That sounds almost like concern.”

“Don't make me regret it.”

Bats studied the folder again. The new information did not fit neatly with the old information. A meeting had been moved. A contact had stopped answering. The location was busier than expected. None of those details proved anything by itself, but together they made the assignment feel wrong.

He closed the folder.

“I'll check the details again before I go.”

“Good.”

“And if they still don't make sense?”

“Then you do not go.”

Bats stood. “Look at us. Communicating.”

Varek did not smile.

Outside, the morning had become afternoon. Bats spent several hours checking the information he had been given. He found small inconsistencies, then larger ones. Nobody could explain why the location had changed. The person who had confirmed the original details was suddenly unavailable.

He called the office.

The receptionist answered.

“Is Varek there?”

“He's in a meeting.”

“Can you tell him the details don't line up?”

“I'll pass it on.”

“Please do.”

Bats ended the call and looked at the street through the window. A group of students walked past, arguing about something on a phone. One of them laughed so hard that the others stopped to look at him.

Bats watched them for a moment.

He wondered what it would be like to have a normal afternoon. To complain about homework. To meet someone after school. To be annoyed about a broken game controller instead of a changed assignment.

His phone vibrated.

A message from Varek: DO NOT PROCEED UNTIL I CALL.

Bats read it twice.

Then the call came.

“Where are you?” Varek asked.

“Near the office.”

“Stay there.”

“What happened?”

“I don't know yet.”

“You don't know?”

“Stay where you are.”

The call ended.

Bats stared at the screen. That was unusual. Varek disliked uncertainty, and he disliked admitting it even more.

A few minutes later, another message arrived. It contained a new location and a short instruction to meet a Network contact there. Bats read it, frowned and called back. No answer.

He should have stayed away. He knew that. The warning was clear.

But the new message claimed the situation was urgent, and the contact was someone he had worked with before. Bats told himself he would only go far enough to understand what was happening. If it felt wrong, he would leave.

He set off.

The location was a commercial building near the edge of the district. The street was quiet, though not empty. A delivery van stood near the loading entrance. A pair of workers carried boxes through a side door. Nothing looked dramatic. Nothing looked like a trap.

That was what made it difficult to decide.

Bats waited across the street and checked his phone. No new message. He looked at the windows, the entrance and the people coming and going. The details did not match what he had been told. The building was supposed to be nearly empty. It wasn't.

He stepped back into the shadow of a doorway.

A voice behind him said, “Bats?”

He turned.

The person who had spoken was not the contact he expected. It was a stranger in a plain jacket, standing a few paces away. Another figure had appeared at the far end of the pavement.

Bats felt his stomach tighten.

“Who are you?” he asked.

“Someone who knows you were coming.”

“That doesn't answer the question.”

The stranger glanced towards the building. “You should come with us.”

“No.”

The answer came out before Bats had time to consider making it polite.

The stranger took a step closer. Bats moved away. The second figure shifted to block the pavement. The street suddenly seemed much narrower than it had a moment earlier.

Bats looked up. There was open air above the buildings. If he could get enough height, he could leave the street behind.

He opened his wings.

A sharp crack split the air.

Pain struck one wing and his balance disappeared. He dropped hard onto the pavement, catching himself with his hands. The wing would not fold properly. It trembled when he tried to move it.

For a moment, all the city sounds seemed far away.

Then the strangers were moving towards him.

Bats pushed himself upright and stumbled towards the nearest open space. He tried to lift off, but the injured wing failed to support him. He managed only a short, uneven leap before landing again.

He ran.

The next few minutes became a confusing series of turns, barriers and shouted instructions. He was tired, frightened and angry with himself. He had been warned. He had noticed the inconsistencies. He had still come.

When he finally stopped, there were people around him and no clear way out. He did not know who had arranged the meeting or why the Network's information had been changed. He only knew that he could no longer fly.

Someone told him to keep still.

Bats looked at the damaged wing, then at the unfamiliar faces.

“I'd love to,” he said, trying to keep his voice steady. “But I have a few questions first.”

Nobody answered.

The world narrowed to the sound of approaching footsteps.

For the first time in years, Bats had no plan that he trusted.

END OF PART 1
