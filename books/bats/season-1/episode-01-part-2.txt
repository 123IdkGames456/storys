BATS: ZOMBIE APOCALYPSE
SEASON 1 — THE BEGINNING
EPISODE 1 — BEFORE EVERYTHING
PART 2

The first thing Bats noticed when he woke was the smell.

It was a mixture of disinfectant, old food and something metallic from the medical room. His head hurt. His injured wing rested awkwardly against his side, wrapped in a thick bandage. He tried to move it and immediately stopped.

“Don't,” said a voice.

Bats turned his head. A prison medic sat at a desk, writing on a form. She was a small fox with tired eyes and a badge that read E. MORROW.

“Where am I?”

“Northbridge Correctional Facility.”

“That's not a hospital.”

“No. It is not.”

“Then why am I here?”

The medic put down her pen. “You were brought in under a transfer order. I don't know the details, and I'm not supposed to ask.”

Bats looked around. The room had a narrow bed, a cabinet and a window too small to climb through. His phone, clothes and everything else were gone.

“Can I leave?”

“No.”

“Can I call someone?”

“Not right now.”

“Can I at least get a better answer than ‘not right now’?”

Morrow gave him a look that suggested she had heard much worse questions that morning.

“You're being processed. The officers will explain what happens next.”

Bats leaned back. He had been in difficult situations before, but this was different. There was no familiar building, no Network supervisor and no one who owed him an explanation. He was a prisoner, and the people in charge had decided that was all he needed to know.

A few minutes later, two officers arrived. One was a tall bear named Harlan. The other, a rabbit with a clipboard, kept checking the paperwork.

“Can you stand?” Harlan asked.

“I can try.”

“Do that slowly.”

Bats swung his legs over the side of the bed. The room tilted slightly. He waited until it steadied, then stood. His injured wing pulled against the bandage. He kept it close to his body.

The rabbit checked a box.

“Name?”

“Bats.”

“Full legal name?”

“Bats.”

The rabbit looked at Harlan.

“That's what people call me,” Bats said. “It's the name you're getting.”

Harlan gestured towards the door. “Move.”

The corridor beyond the medical room was long and painted a dull cream colour. Doors lined both sides. A camera watched from the ceiling. Somewhere farther away, a metal gate opened and closed.

They took him through processing. He was given plain clothes, a thin blanket and a set of rules that were read aloud as if the words had been repeated so many times that nobody expected them to mean anything.

He was assigned to a cell in a quieter section of the facility. The cell contained a bed fixed to the wall, a small sink, a toilet and a narrow window reinforced with thick safety glass. There was a metal frame around the window, and the glass looked almost impossible to damage.

Harlan pointed inside.

“This is yours for now.”

“For now?”

“You'll be told if that changes.”

Bats stepped in.

The door shut behind him with a heavy mechanical sound.

He stood in the centre of the cell and listened.

The prison had its own kind of noise. Footsteps. Keys. Distant voices. Doors. A ventilation system that hummed continuously. It was a building designed to make sure nobody forgot where they were.

Bats sat on the bed.

He thought about Varek.

Had the Network arranged this? Had the people who caught him been working for someone else? Was the organization trying to get him out, or had it decided he was no longer worth the trouble?

He had no answers.

He tried to sleep, but the wing hurt whenever he shifted position. He eventually found a position that was uncomfortable but manageable. The blanket was thin. The room was cold.

The first day passed slowly.

The second day was worse because Bats had begun to understand the routine.

A tray arrived through the slot. An officer checked the cell. A medic looked at his bandage. He was told when to stand, when to sit and when to stop asking questions. Nobody gave him a useful explanation.

He learned the names of a few people by listening to the corridor.

A prisoner two cells away was called Denny. Someone farther down complained about the food every time a tray arrived. An older voice often hummed the same tune in the afternoon.

Bats did not speak much.

He was not sure what he could safely say.

On the third morning, Morrow came to check his wing.

“How does it feel?”

“Like it belongs to somebody else.”

“That isn't a medical description.”

“It's the best one I've got.”

She carefully checked the bandage. Bats watched her expression.

“Will it heal?”

“It needs time. You should avoid putting weight on it.”

“Can I fly?”

“Not now.”

“Later?”

“I can't promise that.”

He looked at the ceiling.

“Great.”

Morrow finished the check and stepped back. “You need to let it rest.”

“I'm in a cell. Rest is most of the schedule.”

She almost smiled. Almost.

Then she left.

That afternoon, the prison routine changed.

It began with an announcement over the speakers. The voice was calm, but the words were difficult to make out. Something about a security procedure. Then the announcement cut off halfway through a sentence.

A few minutes later, a distant alarm sounded.

Bats sat up.

The alarm stopped.

A door slammed somewhere down the corridor.

Then came running footsteps.

He moved to the window in his cell and looked out through the reinforced glass. The narrow view showed part of the exercise yard and a section of the outer wall. A guard crossed the yard quickly, looking over his shoulder.

Another guard followed.

They were not walking like people following a routine.

Bats stepped away from the window.

A voice shouted from the corridor. Another voice answered, too far away to understand. There was a heavy impact, followed by a burst of noise from the speakers.

“Attention. All staff—”

The announcement dissolved into static.

Then the lights flickered.

Bats stood still.

He had learned to notice when a room changed. The prison had been full of predictable sounds. Now the sounds were arriving in the wrong order.

A door opened. Someone shouted for help. Something crashed.

The corridor lights went out.

For several seconds, the only light came through the narrow window.

Then the emergency lights turned on, painting the cell and corridor a dim red.

A guard ran past Bats's cell.

“Get back!” the guard shouted at someone behind him.

A second figure appeared.

At first Bats thought it was another prisoner. It moved with an awkward, uneven step. Its clothes were torn and its head tilted as if it were struggling to understand where it was.

The guard backed away.

“Stay down!”

The figure did not stop.

The guard tried to move around it. There was a struggle, a loud crash against the wall and then the guard disappeared from Bats's view.

Bats stared through the bars.

He had seen people injured. He had seen people frightened. He had seen people behave strangely under pressure.

This was not like any of those things.

The figure slowly turned towards the cells.

Its eyes did not seem to focus properly. Its movements were clumsy, but it kept coming.

A door farther down the corridor opened. A prisoner shouted. The figure changed direction.

Bats stepped back.

“What is happening?” he called.

Nobody answered.

More noise came from the other side of the prison. The emergency lights flickered again. A series of heavy bangs travelled through the building.

Then the figure reached his cell.

It stopped outside the bars.

Bats held his breath.

The creature leaned towards the gap. Its mouth opened, and a low, rough sound came from its throat. It reached through the bars, searching for him.

Bats moved away from the door.

“Okay,” he whispered. “That is new.”

The creature struck the bars.

The metal rattled.

Bats looked towards the cell door. It was locked. He looked at the window. The glass was thick, reinforced and set into a metal frame. The only other opening was the narrow slot in the door.

The creature struck the bars again.

A warning light flashed above the corridor. Somewhere, a siren began to sound and did not stop.

Bats tried the door anyway. It did not move.

He pulled at the handle, then stepped back as the creature pushed an arm farther through the bars.

He had no way to fly. His injured wing was almost useless, and the other could not carry him alone. He had no equipment, no phone and no idea whether anyone in the building was still in control.

He looked at the window again.

The reinforced glass was designed to resist impacts. It was not designed to be broken by a prisoner with an injured wing.

Bats knew that.

He also knew the creature outside was not going to leave just because he asked politely.

He took a breath and moved towards the window.

The pain in his wing was already making him dizzy. He pressed his shoulder against the wall, trying to steady himself.

“Sorry,” he muttered, though he wasn't sure whether he meant the wing or himself.

The creature hit the bars again.

Bats made his decision.

He turned his body, protected his face as best he could and drove the injured wing against the reinforced glass.

The first impact made the frame shudder.

The second sent a deep vibration through the wall.

He nearly fell. His vision blurred. The wing felt worse than before, but the glass remained in place.

Outside the cell, the creature kept reaching through the bars.

Bats looked at the window.

“One more,” he whispered.

He gathered what strength he had left and struck again.

A sharp crack travelled across the pane.

The glass had not fallen, but a fracture spread through it.

Bats stared.

For one brief second, he felt hope.

Then the creature slammed against the cell door.

The door shook.

Bats backed away, breathing hard. His injured wing hung at an awkward angle. He knew the next attempt could cost him more than he could afford to lose.

The door rattled again.

He turned back to the window.

The prison speakers crackled.

A voice, faint and distorted, said something about an evacuation. Then came a loud bang and the announcement ended.

Bats took one last step towards the glass.

He did not know what waited on the other side. He only knew that staying in the cell was no longer an option.
